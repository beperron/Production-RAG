Understanding the Search System

A plain-language brief on the North Carolina child-welfare law & policy search tool — what it is, what it reads, how it was built, and how it is used.

In one sentence: this is a research tool that looks up the actual North Carolina statutes and agency policies that apply to a question, then writes a short answer grounded in those exact sources and showing where each point came from — and it declines to answer when the sources don't support one.

1. What "RAG" means, without the jargon

The technology is called Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). It sounds technical, but the idea is simple and can be pictured as two people working together:

A librarian (the retrieval part) — given a question, it finds the handful of passages from the real law and policy documents that most likely answer it.

A careful writer (the generation part) — it reads only those retrieved passages and writes a short, plain answer, quoting and citing them. It is instructed not to rely on memory, and to say "the sources don't support this" rather than guess.

The reason this matters: an ordinary chatbot answers from a blur of everything it once read and can state wrong things confidently. This system is deliberately constrained — it must show its work by pointing back to the specific source document, section, and page.

2. The corpus — what the system reads

The system's knowledge is limited to a defined library of public North Carolina child-welfare authority. It is not connected to the open internet and contains no confidential records. Two bodies of material:

The law

North Carolina General Statutes, Chapter 7B — the Juvenile Code covering abuse, neglect, dependency, custody, permanency, and termination of parental rights — plus supporting sections of the NC Administrative Code.

The policy

The North Carolina DHHS child-welfare policy manuals, protocols, forms, and guidance that county agencies are expected to follow — intake, safety and risk assessment, in-home services, permanency planning, and related standards.

Scale of the policy library: 4,157 pages across 475 documents of NCDHHS child-welfare policy, plus the curated Chapter 7B statutes.

The statute set is curated (the Chapter 7B core and its supporting sections), not the entire General Statutes. Everything in the library is public, official material.

3. How the library was prepared

Raw government PDFs are not directly searchable in a reliable way, so each document went through a one-time preparation, shown in the figure below:

  1. Converted to clean text while keeping the original page numbers, so any result can be traced back to the exact page.
  2. Split into passages (a section or a few paragraphs at a time), and each passage was given a SHA-256 fingerprint — a tamper-evident code that changes if even one character of the source is altered, so the text can be shown to be unchanged.
  3. Indexed two ways: by keyword (exact words, like a statute number) and by meaning (so a plain-English question finds the right provision even when it uses different words than the statute).

Figure 1. How the North Carolina law & policy search system is prepared and used. The top row (done once) converts official public sources into a searchable knowledge base; the bottom row (run for every question) retrieves the best-matching passages and drafts an answer built only from them, with citations. The system is limited to public law and policy and declines to answer when the retrieved passages do not support one.

4. How it is used

For any given question the system offers two things:

  • Search — returns the most relevant statute sections and policy passages, each with a full citation (source, section, page) and a link to the official text.
  • Grounded answer — an optional short written answer that pulls together the retrieved passages, with inline citations to each. If the passages don't contain enough to answer, it says so instead of inventing an answer.

Under the hood, the "librarian" step combines the keyword and meaning searches and re-ranks the top passages so the best ones rise to the top. Independent testing of this step found the governing statute in the top three results about nine times in ten, and the correct policy document in the top three about 96% of the time. (Details are in the companion quality report.)

5. Why it is designed to be trustworthy

Shows its sources

Every result and every sentence of an answer points to a specific public document, section, and page.

Tamper-evident

The SHA-256 fingerprint on each passage lets anyone confirm the quoted text matches the official source, unchanged.

Declines when unsure

If the retrieved passages don't support an answer, it says so rather than guessing — but still shows the retrieved results, so the reader can judge the sources directly.

Public-only, walled off

The system can only reach the public law-and-policy library. It cannot access confidential case records; that boundary is enforced in the software itself.

6. What it is not

  • It is not legal advice and not a substitute for a lawyer's judgment. The official source it links to remains the controlling text.
  • It does not contain or interpret any specific case file — it describes only what North Carolina law and policy require in general.
  • Its library is curated; a question about material outside the indexed NC statutes and policy will not resolve.
  • Retrieval is strong but not perfect — occasionally the exact provision sits at result two or three behind a closely related section, so results are worth a quick scan rather than reading only the first hit.

Bottom line for the reader. Think of this as a fast, well-organized research assistant for the public North Carolina child-welfare rulebook: it finds the governing law or policy, quotes it with a verifiable citation, and is built to say "I'm not sure" rather than fabricate. A qualified reviewer still confirms currency and how any provision applies.
